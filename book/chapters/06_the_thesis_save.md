# The Thesis Save

## April 1996

Jennifer Wu was in her third year of a PhD program at the University of Arizona, studying fault kinematics in the Catalina Mountains. Her dissertation focused on measuring fault orientations across a shear zone to understand the strain patterns during mountain building.

She needed 87 measurements. Precise orientations of fault surfaces, slickenlines, fracture patterns. Three weeks of field work in rough terrain.

Day 18 of her field session, she had collected all 87 measurements. They were stored on her laptop—a Toshiba notebook computer that had cost $2,400 and represented a significant chunk of her student budget.

The data was backed up. On a floppy disk. In her laptop bag.

She was hiking back to camp, crossing a creek on stepping stones. The creek was running high from spring snowmelt.

She slipped.

The laptop bag, slung over her shoulder, swung forward with her momentum. It hit the water.

She caught herself. Didn't fall in. But the bag was submerged for three, maybe four seconds before she yanked it up.

"No no no no no."

She ran to camp. Unzipped the bag.

Water poured out.

The laptop was soaked. She tried to power it on.

Nothing.

She tried the floppy disk. Water had seeped into the bag. The disk was wet.

She put it in her backup laptop (borrowed from a labmate). The disk couldn't be read. Magnetic media and water don't mix.

Three years of work. 87 measurements. Gone.

Jennifer sat on a rock and cried for an hour.

Then she remembered.

## The Backup Plan

Three weeks ago, before leaving for the field, her advisor had lent her his GeoCalc DX. "Try it out," he'd said. "Tell me if it's worth the money."

She'd been using it to double-check her Brunton compass readings. A backup, really. The laptop was her primary data store. The GeoCalc was just... supplementary. A toy, kind of. Definitely not her main system.

But every measurement she'd entered into her field database, she'd also entered into the GeoCalc.

All 87 of them.

On the cartridge. In battery-backed SRAM.

She pulled the GeoCalc from her backpack. It had been in there during the creek incident. Slightly damp on the outside but the Game Boy itself was fine.

She powered it on.

LOAD PROJECT.

Her project loaded: "CATALINA_FAULTS"

87 measurements.

Every single one.

Trend, plunge, type, notes. Everything.

She borrowed another laptop the next day (drove two hours to Tucson, begged a labmate). The GeoCalc DX came with a link cable and PC software. She'd never used it before—hadn't needed to.

She connected the cable. Launched GeoCalc Manager on Windows.

UPLOAD FROM CARTRIDGE.

12 seconds later, all 87 measurements were on the PC.

Export to CSV.

She opened it in Excel. Every measurement. Perfect data.

Her entire dataset. Intact.

## The Reality

The thesis save story was mostly luck.

GeoCalc's battery-backed SRAM was designed for convenience, not disaster recovery. Users could save their data, power off, and resume work later. Like a field notebook, but digital.

Nobody at GeoStructure had explicitly thought: "This will save PhD dissertations when laptops die."

They'd just built something reliable because the Game Boy was reliable.

But reliability has consequences.

When everything else failed—laptop, backup disk, primary storage—the one system that kept working was the thing designed not to break.

The Game Boy cartridge, with its simple battery-backed RAM, outlasted the sophisticated computer.

Sometimes that's all that matters.

Until it was the only thing left.

## The Lesson

Sarah summarized it in a talk at GSA in 1998:

"Field data is precious. You can't go back in time to re-measure an outcrop. You can't recreate observations. Digital backup is essential, but digital systems fail.

"The thesis save taught us something: redundancy matters. Not fancy redundancy. Simple, robust redundancy.

"Three storage systems beat one sophisticated system. Pencil in field notebook. Computer database. GeoCalc cartridge. Three independent copies. If one fails, you have two others.

"Jennifer had all three. The laptop failed. The floppy disk failed. The cartridge survived.

"That's not because cartridges are magic. It's because they're simple. Battery-backed RAM. No moving parts. Sealed against moisture. Designed for children to abuse.

"Complexity fails in the field. Simplicity survives.

"Build simple, reliable things. They might save someone's PhD."

## The Meta-Story

The thesis save became part of GeoCalc's identity.

New features were evaluated against it: "Would this have saved Jennifer's data? If not, does it matter?"

Marketing focused on reliability over sophistication: "Three years of research. One Game Boy cartridge."

Competitors offered fancier features. GeoCalc offered: it doesn't break.

When GeoStructure designed later products—Palm version, Windows Mobile, iOS/Android—data persistence was always priority one.

Auto-save. Cloud backup. Multiple export formats. Export early, export often.

All because one PhD student's laptop fell in a creek, and the backup system nobody thought about turned out to be the only system that mattered.

## Jennifer's Defense

Jennifer Wu successfully defended her dissertation in June 1996.

Her advisor, Dr. Hernandez, opened the defense with: "This work represents three years of intensive field research. It nearly didn't happen. I'd like to take a moment to thank Nintendo, who unknowingly contributed to structural geology by making durable portable computers."

Laughter from the committee.

Jennifer graduated. Got a postdoc at Stanford. Eventually became a professor at UT Austin.

She still has the GeoCalc DX that saved her data. It sits on her office bookshelf, next to her bound dissertation.

When students ask about it, she tells the story.

"This is the device that taught me: always have multiple backups. And at least one of them should be too dumb to fail."

Good advice for life, not just geology.

They met at the same climbing gym cafe where Bob had pitched the Game Boy idea to Maggie in 1992.

Jennifer brought the GeoCalc DX. Still worked.

"I've never changed the battery," she said. "The SRAM battery. It's been 20 years. The data is probably gone."

Bob turned it on.

LOAD PROJECT.

CATALINA_FAULTS.

87 measurements loaded.

"CR2032 batteries last 20+ years in low-drain applications," Bob said. "As long as you didn't overwrite it, the data's still there."

Jennifer looked at the screen. The same measurements she'd collected in 1996. Preserved perfectly.

"How many people," she asked, "can say their 20-year-old data is still readable on the original hardware?"

"Not many," Bob admitted.

"Thank you," Jennifer said. "For building something that lasts. For building something that saved not just my data, but my career. My confidence. My belief that I could be a scientist."

"You saved your own career," Bob said. "We just made a tool. You did the geology."

"You made a tool that didn't fail when I needed it most. That matters."

They talked for two hours. About structural geology, about technology, about what lasts and what doesn't.

As she left, Jennifer said: "I tell my students this story every year. Some think it's about data backup. Some think it's about hardware reliability. But really, it's about this: make things that work. Not things that look impressive. Not things that have every feature. Things that work when it matters."

"That's all we ever tried to do," Bob said.

"You succeeded."

Bob watched her leave, still carrying the 20-year-old Game Boy device that had saved her thesis.

Some tools outlive their makers.

Some tools become part of people's stories.

That's enough.
