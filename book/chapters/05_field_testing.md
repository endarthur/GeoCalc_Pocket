# Field Testing and the Nintendo Letter

## March 1994: The Morrison Test

Sarah took five beta units to the Morrison Formation outcrops west of Denver. Classic dinosaur country—mudstone and sandstone layers tilted and folded by ancient tectonic forces. She knew this area intimately. Had measured it a dozen times with traditional methods.

This was the real test.

Day one was perfect. Sunny, 60°F, light wind. Sarah set up at an outcrop, pulled out her Brunton compass and the GeoCalc.

Measure bedding orientation: 042°, 28° NE.

Record in field notebook (pencil, paper, old school).

Enter into GeoCalc (D-pad, A button, new school).

The point appeared on the stereonet.

She moved to the next exposure. Measured. Recorded. Plotted.

Ten measurements. Twenty. Thirty.

A pattern emerged on the stereonet—poles clustering around a great circle, indicating cylindrical folding. Exactly what she expected from previous work.

She compared the GeoCalc stereonet to her hand-plotted version from last year.

Identical.

"Okay," she said to herself. "Day one: success."

Day two was a thunderstorm.

## The Rain Test

Sarah hadn't planned to work in the rain. But the storm rolled in at 10 AM, and she was three miles from her truck, and the outcrop she needed to measure was right there.

She'd test the water resistance. Accidentally.

The Game Boy got wet. Not submerged, but rain-wet. Water on the screen, water on the buttons.

It kept working.

Sarah measured bedding through the rain, squinting at the screen, water dripping off her hard hat.

The GeoCalc kept plotting. The buttons still responded. The screen was harder to read (reflective LCD + water = glare), but functional.

She made it back to her truck, dried off the Game Boy with a towel, tested it.

Still worked.

She included this in her report: "Survived moderate rain. Screen visibility reduced but acceptable. Recommend users carry in waterproof case but device appears robust to incidental water exposure."

Bob read this and added a note to the manual: "GeoCalc Pocket is splash-resistant but not waterproof. Do not submerge. Do use in field conditions."

## The Glove Test

Cold-weather testing came in April, up in the mountains near Idaho Springs.

Temperature: 35°F. Sarah wore Mechanix work gloves—standard field gear.

She tried to use the GeoCalc.

Failed.

The buttons were too small. The D-pad required too much precision. With gloves on, she kept hitting the wrong buttons, entering incorrect values.

"This is a problem," she reported.

Lisa and Kevin redesigned the input handling.

New system:
- Larger "hit zones" for button presses
- More forgiveness in D-pad readings
- SELECT button as tool toggle—easy to press with gloved thumb
- Visual feedback for every input (screen flashes, confirmation beeps)

Sarah tested the new version in May. Same location, same gloves.

"Much better," she reported. "Still not as easy as bare hands, but functional. I can use this in winter field work."

The glove test became part of their standard QA process. If it didn't work with gloves, it didn't ship.

## The Sunlight Problem

The original Game Boy screen was reflective but not great in direct sun.

Sarah's report: "At midday in summer, screen washes out. Stereonet grid barely visible. Can still plot but requires shading screen with hand."

James's solution: Bolder grid lines, higher contrast. The grid became thicker than aesthetically ideal, but readable in harsh sunlight.

They tested it. Sarah took a unit to a south-facing outcrop at noon in June.

"I can read it. It's not perfect, but I can read it."

Good enough.

Field usability beat aesthetic perfection. Again.

## The Drop Test

The official drop test was Sarah's idea.

"If we're marketing this as field equipment," she said, "we need to prove it's field-worthy."

Bob agreed. "What's the test?"

"Six feet. Onto concrete. If it survives, we can claim it's field-ready."

They set up in the parking lot behind the office. Kevin held the Game Boy at six feet (his height). Dropped it.

The Game Boy hit concrete. Bounced. Landed screen-down.

Kevin picked it up. Powered it on.

It worked.

They tested five more units. All survived. One had a hairline crack in the case. All functioned perfectly.

"The Game Boy is tougher than our confidence in it," James observed.

The drop test became part of their marketing. Photos of Sarah dropping units. Testimonials from field geologists who'd abused their devices and had them keep working.

Years later, units would be documented surviving:
- 40-foot fall down a mine shaft
- Being run over by a field vehicle
- Submerged in a creek (dried out, kept working)
- Used as a hammer (don't ask)

The Game Boy's durability was their secret weapon.

## January 1995: The Nintendo Letter

Three months post-launch. 450 units sold. Things were going well.

Then the letter arrived.

**From:** Nintendo of America, Legal Department
**Subject:** Unauthorized Use of Trademark

> Dear GeoStructure Systems,
>
> It has come to our attention that your product "GeoCalc Pocket" makes unauthorized use of the Game Boy trademark in marketing materials and packaging. Game Boy is a registered trademark of Nintendo of America Inc.
>
> Please be advised that use of Nintendo's trademarks without authorization may constitute trademark infringement under federal and state law. We request that you immediately:
>
> 1. Cease all use of "Game Boy" in marketing materials
> 2. Remove references to "Game Boy" from packaging
> 3. Clarify that your product is not endorsed by Nintendo
>
> Please respond within 30 days indicating your compliance...

Bob read it twice. Then called an emergency meeting.

## Three Days of Panic

**Day One:** "Are we being sued?"

Maggie read the letter again. "I don't think so. This is a cease-and-desist. They're warning us, not suing us. Yet."

"Can they shut us down?" James asked.

"If they want to make our lives difficult, yes," Maggie said. "They're Nintendo. We're nobody."

"Our entire marketing is 'works with Game Boy,'" Sarah pointed out. "How do we market this without mentioning the platform?"

Nobody had an answer.

**Day Two:** Legal consultation.

Cost: $2,500 they didn't really have.

The lawyer's assessment: "You're probably fine. This is descriptive use—you're describing compatibility, not claiming to BE Nintendo or that Nintendo endorses you. But if they want to press it, they can. And they have deeper pockets."

"What do we do?"

"Add disclaimers. Make it very clear you're not affiliated with Nintendo. Stop using their logo. Use text like 'compatible with Game Boy systems' rather than 'Game Boy software.' Hope they accept that."

"And if they don't?"

"Then you either fight—which you can't afford—or stop selling."

**Day Three:** Bob calls Nintendo.

He didn't tell Maggie first. Just looked up Nintendo of America's legal department and called.

The conversation:

**Nintendo lawyer:** "Mr. Kuwahara. Thank you for calling. We need to protect our trademark."

**Bob:** "I understand. We're not trying to infringe. We're making professional geological instruments that happen to run on your hardware. We think it actually makes your platform look more serious—you know, scientists using Game Boys in the field."

**Nintendo lawyer:** "...That's an interesting angle."

**Bob:** "We're just a tiny company selling to geologists. Seven people. We're not competition. We're not making games. We're not claiming any affiliation. We just need to tell customers that our software works on Game Boy systems."

**Nintendo lawyer:** [long pause] "Send us your marketing materials. We'll review them. If you're not implying endorsement, and you add proper disclaimers, we can probably work with this."

**Bob:** "Thank you. We'll send them today."

**Nintendo lawyer:** "And Mr. Kuwahara? Off the record? My cousin is a geology major at University of Washington. He's going to think this is cool."

**Bob:** "Tell him we have educational discounts."

## The Resolution

New disclaimer on all materials:

> Game Boy is a trademark of Nintendo of America Inc. GeoCalc Pocket is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or sponsored by Nintendo. This product is designed to be compatible with Game Boy systems.

Prominent placement. Clear language. No ambiguity.

They sent samples to Nintendo's legal department.

Three weeks later: "This is acceptable. Thank you for your cooperation."

Crisis averted.

Bob never heard from Nintendo again. Years later, through back channels, he heard that Nintendo's legal team thought it was "kind of cool" that their hardware was being used professionally. They just needed to protect the trademark officially.

The disclaimer stayed on every piece of GeoCalc marketing for the next 16 years.

## The Competitor That Wasn't

In September 1997, an Australian startup called GeoPro announced GeoPro GB—a Game Boy stereonet program with more features than GeoCalc.

On paper, it was better:
- Rose diagrams
- Both Schmidt AND Wulff projections
- Contouring
- Fancier UI with animated transitions
- "Professional Grade Field Analysis"

Price: $399 (vs GeoCalc's $299)

Maggie saw the announcement at a conference. "We have competition."

Bob wasn't worried. "Let's see if they ship."

They shipped. November 1997.

By November 1999, they were gone.

**Why it failed:**

1. **Confusing UI** - Too many features crammed into limited screen space
2. **Crashes** - Memory leaks caused freezes after 30-40 measurements
3. **Poor documentation** - 12-page manual vs GeoCalc's 64-page guide
4. **No support** - Email went unanswered for weeks
5. **No printer support** - Even after GB Printer launched
6. **Price** - $100 more than GeoCalc for buggier software

The company burned through $200K in 18 months. Sold maybe 200 units total.

Maggie called the founder—Paul Wong, geologist from University of Sydney—in December 1999.

"Paul? Maggie Chen from GeoStructure. I heard GeoPro shut down. I'm sorry."

"Thanks. We couldn't compete. Your product is just... better."

"What are you doing with the remaining inventory?"

"147 units in a warehouse. I'll probably trash them."

"Don't trash them. What if we buy them? We'll donate to universities in developing countries. You get some money back, the units get used."

The deal: $3,000 for 147 units ($20 each).

GeoStructure donated them to geology programs in Mexico, Philippines, Chile, India.

The PR impact: "GeoStructure Systems Donates Competitors' Inventory to Educational Programs"

Paul became a customer in 2002 (bought GeoCalc Advance).

Competition existed. Competition failed.

GeoCalc survived because it worked, because they supported it, because they listened to users.

Sometimes that's enough.

## The AGU Booth (1998)

By 1998, GeoCalc had sold about 3,000 units. Not huge numbers, but solid for a niche product.

Maggie booked a booth at the American Geophysical Union conference in San Francisco. Professional booth, banner, demo units, the works.

The pitch: "Plot stereonets in the field. Real-time structural analysis. Proven reliability."

Geologists would stop, skeptical. "Is that a Game Boy?"

"Yes."

"For geology?"

"Yes."

"Does it work?"

"Try it."

They'd try it. Enter measurements. Watch points plot in real time.

The skepticism would fade.

"This is... actually useful."

"That's why we built it."

Most common questions:
1. "How accurate is it?" (±1 degree)
2. "How long does the battery last?" (30+ hours)
3. "What if I drop it?" (It's a Game Boy. It doesn't break.)
4. "Can I export the data?" (Not yet. But we're working on it.)

That last question came up constantly. Data export was the #1 feature request.

Bob took notes. "We need a link cable adapter. PC connection. Export to Excel, StereoWin, whatever they use."

That would become GeoCalc DX.

But first, something happened that would become their most famous story.

The thesis save.
