# Preface {.unnumbered}

In December 1991, a nine-year-old boy named Danny dropped his Nintendo Game Boy down a flight of stairs. The device hit concrete, bounced twice, and kept playing *Tetris*. This unremarkable moment of childhood clumsiness would, within three years, change how structural geologists work in the field.

Danny's uncle, Bob Kuwahara, was watching. Bob was a geophysicist turned software engineer, and he was desperate. The company he'd co-founded with two colleagues was four months from bankruptcy. Their latest product—a custom-built electronic compass for field geology—had a 15% return rate because the sensor would drift after a few days. Meanwhile, this $89 toy from Nintendo could survive a six-foot drop onto concrete without a scratch.

"What are we doing?" Bob wrote in his engineering notebook that night.

Six weeks later, he convinced his partners to bet the company's remaining runway on an absurd idea: build professional scientific instruments using Game Boy hardware.

---

This is the story of GeoCalc Pocket and the small company that created it. It's a story about constraint-driven innovation, about finding the right tool rather than the most sophisticated one, and about the strange places where technology and human need intersect.

GeoStructure Systems never became a household name. You won't find them in the annals of great tech companies. But between 1994 and 2013, they sold over 74,000 units of geological software on Nintendo hardware, changed how structural geology is taught in universities, and built something that genuinely mattered to a small community of scientists.

I first encountered a GeoCalc unit in 2003, in a field camp in Montana. My professor pulled out what looked like a Game Boy Advance SP, plotted measurements on it while standing at the outcrop, and printed a stereonet using a Game Boy Printer that she tucked into her field notebook. I was fascinated—not just by the improbability of using gaming hardware for serious science, but by how *well it worked*.

Years later, when I began researching the history of niche computing devices, GeoCalc kept coming up. Every structural geologist over 40 seemed to have a story. Some still used their units. One professor at UC Berkeley told me his original 1996 GeoCalc DX had plotted over 10,000 measurements and still worked perfectly.

The more I researched, the more I realized this wasn't just a story about a weird product. It was a story about people: stubborn engineers, field geologists who needed tools that actually worked, and the intersection of necessity and creativity.

---

This book draws on:

- The original design documents from GeoStructure Systems (graciously shared by the founders)
- Interviews with the development team, users, and beta testers
- Contemporary reviews and academic papers citing GeoCalc
- Technical analysis of the hardware and software
- Field notebooks from geologists who used the tools

Some dialogue has been reconstructed from participants' memories and notes. Some details have been compressed or simplified for narrative flow. But the core story—of how a failing company pivoted to building geology tools on Game Boy hardware, nearly went bankrupt (again), and ultimately succeeded—is true.

---

Why tell this story now?

Because we live in an era obsessed with disruption, unicorns, and scale. GeoCalc was none of those things. It was a niche product for a tiny market, built by a handful of people who were just trying to solve a problem.

It sold 74,000 units over 19 years—a rounding error for Nintendo, a failure by Silicon Valley standards. But it changed structural geology education, saved at least one PhD thesis, and possibly prevented a mine collapse.

It succeeded because it worked. Not because it was sophisticated, or venture-funded, or marketed brilliantly. It worked in the field, where most technology fails.

That lesson feels worth preserving.

**A Note on the Characters**

The characters in this story are fictional, but they're based on the kinds of people who actually built niche technical products in the 1990s. If you worked at a small engineering company during that era—staying late to debug hardware, arguing over feature priorities, celebrating small victories—you may recognize the dynamics.

The personalities, motivations, and relationships are crafted to serve the story, but the engineering challenges, financial pressures, and technical decisions reflect real constraints of the period.

---

**A Note on the Technology**

Parts II and III include technical details about Game Boy programming, stereonet mathematics, and field geology. I've tried to make these accessible to non-specialists while preserving accuracy. If you're not interested in how fixed-point arithmetic works on a Z80-like processor, feel free to skim those sections—the human story remains intact.

For readers who *are* interested, the appendices include deeper technical dives, code samples, and specifications.

---

This is a story about making something that matters, even if only to a few hundred geologists with strange taste in field equipment.

It's about what happens when you can't build the perfect tool, so you build the one that actually works.

It's about a Game Boy that wouldn't break.

Let's begin.
