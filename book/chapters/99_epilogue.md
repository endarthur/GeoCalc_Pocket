# Epilogue {.unnumbered}

## December 2023

Bob Kuwahara, age 65, retired from consulting three years ago. He spends his days building tube amplifiers, roasting coffee, and occasionally teaching embedded systems at CU Boulder when they need a guest lecturer.

His home office overlooks the foothills west of Golden, Colorado. Same area where GeoStructure Systems started in Maggie's garage in 1987. Same mountains he's looked at for forty years.

On his desk:

**The cracked Game Boy** from the 4 AM crisis in 1992. Screen still cracked. Still works. Mounted on a stand with a brass plaque: "Sometimes you just have to throw things at walls."

**The napkin** from the January 1992 pitch. Framed. Maggie's handwriting: "GEOCALC - Game Boy Stereonet Plotter."

**Serial #001** from the 1994 production run. First GeoCalc Pocket ever manufactured. Battery died years ago. Bob hasn't replaced it. It sits there, a relic.

**Jennifer Wu's dissertation.** Bound copy from 1996. Dedication page still makes him smile.

**A photo** from AGU 2010. Maggie talking to Michael Rodriguez. Bob captured the moment just as Michael said, "You changed my life." You can see Maggie's eyes welling up.

Bob is writing. Not code. Not documentation. A blog post. He still keeps a technical blog, read by maybe 200 people. Mostly former colleagues, a few retro computing enthusiasts, some geology students who discovered his posts while researching GeoCalc history.

Today's post:

---

**December 26, 2023**

Thirty-two years ago today, my nephew Danny dropped a Game Boy down the stairs.

I've told this story a hundred times. At conferences, in interviews, in the book that someone wrote about us (still weird seeing my life as a chapter). It's become the origin myth. The moment everything changed.

But I've never written about what happened *after*. After the company sold. After we moved to smartphones. After the Game Boy era ended.

So here it is. The epilogue nobody asked for.

---

**What Happened to the People:**

**Danny** (the nephew who dropped the Game Boy) is 41 now. He's a geologist. I like to think there's a connection, but he insists he chose geology independently. He uses GeoCalc on his iPhone. Has never used a Game Boy for geology. Thinks it's hilarious that his childhood clumsiness became family legend.

**Jennifer Wu** (thesis-save Jennifer) is a professor at UT Austin. Associate Professor of Structural Geology. Still has the GeoCalc DX that saved her dissertation. Uses it as a teaching prop: "This is the device that taught me to always have multiple backups."

**Michael Rodriguez** (the student from AGU 2010) is Dr. Michael Rodriguez now. Postdoc at Stanford. Published six papers on fold kinematics. Emailed me last year: "Defended my thesis. Thanked you in the acknowledgments. Hope that's okay." Of course it was okay.

**Dr. Carlos Hernandez** (University of Texas, stubborn SP holdout) finally retired his Advance SP in 2018. Battery-backed SRAM finally died after 14 years. He framed it. Hung it in his office. Next to his Brunton compass collection.

**Maggie** is 65, technically retired, still works 20 hours a week for Trimble as "advisor emeritus." Still does field work every summer. Still collects Brunton compasses (up to 63). Drives a 2019 Toyota 4Runner (has 60K miles already). Will probably die in the field at age 90, measuring a fault. Would want it that way.

**James** is 61, retired last year. Finally. Angela (his wife, still a pediatrician) insisted. They're traveling. He emails photos from field sites around the world. "Saw a great fold in the Dolomites. Wished I had a GeoCalc." I remind him there's an iPhone app. He says it's not the same.

**Sarah** is 63, still Director of Field Testing at Trimble. Refuses to retire. "I'll retire when things stop breaking. So, never." Still lives in her van. Completed her 7th continent ultramarathon last year (Antarctica, finally). Has tested over 5,000 devices in her career. Possibly the most destructive person in tech.

**Lisa** is 54, wrote three more books after "Designing for Dirt." Teaches full-time at ArtCenter now. Her children's books are charming (I bought them for my grandkids). She still designs UX for Trimble part-time. "Can't quit completely. Too interesting."

**Kevin** is 52, still at Trimble, still building weird things. Latest project: AR geology using HoloLens. Will probably work until he's 80. Some people retire, some people just keep making cool stuff.

---

**What Happened to the Technology:**

GeoCalc still exists. iOS, Android, Windows. Used by thousands of geologists worldwide. Trimble kept the brand, kept the team (mostly), kept the philosophy.

The newest version (GeoCalc 2023) has features we never dreamed of in 1994:
- GPS integration
- Cloud sync
- AI-assisted feature detection
- Photogrammetry integration
- AR visualization
- Export to 37 different formats

It's sophisticated. Professional. Modern.

It's also missing something the Game Boy version had: weirdness.

Nobody writes articles about "geology app for smartphone." That's normal. Expected. Boring.

The Game Boy was weird. And weird mattered.

---

**What I Learned:**

I learned that constraints drive innovation. We built GeoCalc on Game Boy because we couldn't afford custom hardware. That constraint forced creativity. Made us find solutions we wouldn't have considered otherwise.

I learned that simple beats sophisticated in the field. Every feature we added, we asked: "Will this work when you're cold, tired, wet, and frustrated?" If no, we didn't add it.

I learned that durability is a feature. Game Boys didn't break. That wasn't a side effect. That was the core value proposition.

I learned that niche markets matter. We never sold millions of units. Didn't need to. Thousands of geologists buying reliable tools was enough. Sustainable. Profitable. Meaningful.

I learned that tools outlive their makers. My code from 1994 still runs (in emulators, in collectors' systems, in that one professor in New Zealand who refuses to upgrade). Will outlive me. That's... weird to think about.

Most importantly: I learned that the people matter more than the technology.

Jennifer's saved thesis matters more than the link cable technology.

Michael's "I finally get it" moment matters more than the stereonet algorithm.

Carlos's stubborn refusal to upgrade matters more than any new feature.

The technology was just the enabler. The people were the point.

---

**What I'd Do Differently:**

Nothing. And everything.

Would I start earlier on smartphone versions? Maybe. But we didn't have the resources in 1994. Timing was what it was.

Would I skip the DS? No. Deciding NOT to build it was the right choice. Constraints include saying no.

Would I change the manufacturing disasters? Can't change those. They taught us resilience.

Would I skip AeroGeo 3D? Absolutely not. Kevin needed to build it. Some things aren't about ROI.

Actually, one thing: I wish we'd documented more. Kept better records. Saved more emails. Taken more photos.

I have the technical artifacts (napkin, cracked Game Boy, serial #001). But I wish I had more of the human moments. The conversations. The late-night debugging sessions. The field tests that went wrong. The coffee shop arguments about UI design.

Memory fades. Documents last.

If you're building something interesting, document it. Not just the code. The process. The people. The chaos.

Future you will thank present you.

---

**What Happens Next:**

Nothing dramatic.

Trimble will keep selling GeoCalc. It's a solid product line. Profitable. Sustainable.

The Game Boy versions are museum pieces now. Collectibles. Occasionally, one shows up on eBay for $300-500. Collectors want them. Not to use. To own a piece of weird tech history.

The last active GeoCalc SP user (that I know of) is Dr. Susan Martinez at Oregon State. Uses it for teaching. "Students need to see what field tools used to be like. Appreciate modern tech more when they've used a Game Boy with a cracked screen."

Eventually, the batteries will all die. The flash memory will degrade. The hardware will fail.

But the data will persist. Thousands of stereonet plots in field notebooks. Hundreds of published papers. Dozens of PhD dissertations.

The measurements live on. Georeferenced. Archived. Preserved.

That's what we built. Not just software. Not just hardware. A bridge between field observation and understanding. A way to make the invisible visible.

That bridge still exists. The platform changed. The idea didn't.

---

**Final Thoughts:**

Sometimes I wonder: What if Danny hadn't dropped that Game Boy?

What if I'd been watching TV instead? What if the Game Boy had broken (impossible, but hypothetically)? What if I'd dismissed the idea?

GeoStructure Systems would have gone bankrupt in 1992. I'd have found another job. Maggie, James, the team—we'd all have scattered.

Someone else might have eventually built geology software for mobile devices. Probably on Palm Pilot. Probably more sophisticated. Definitely more normal.

But it wouldn't have been weird. Wouldn't have been Game Boys in the field. Wouldn't have been a dropped toy inspiring a company pivot. Wouldn't have been this story.

And this story mattered.

Not because we sold millions of units. Not because we got rich (the acquisition was nice, but I was never in it for money).

Because we proved something: Weird ideas can work. Constraints drive innovation. Reliable beats sophisticated. Niche markets matter.

Most importantly: Sometimes toys make better tools than tools.

---

**To Danny:**

Thanks for dropping that Game Boy.

Thanks for being a clumsy nine-year-old.

Thanks for setting off a chain of events that defined twenty years of my career and helped thousands of geologists.

Your Game Boy is still in my office. Still cracked. Still works.

Just like our company: broken in places, but fundamentally sound.

---

**To everyone who bought a GeoCalc:**

Thank you. For trusting a weird idea. For using a video game console professionally. For emailing us bug reports, feature requests, and occasionally stories about saved theses and changed careers.

You made this work. Not us. We just built the tool. You used it in ways we never imagined.

That's the greatest compliment a toolmaker can receive.

---

**To the next generation:**

Find your dropped Game Boy moment. The moment when you see something ordinary and think: "What if...?"

Chase that thought. Build the weird thing. Ignore the skeptics (including yourself).

Sometimes the best ideas are the ones that make no sense. Until they do.

Sometimes the best tools are the ones nobody else thought to build.

Sometimes toys are better than industrial equipment.

Pay attention. See possibilities. Build things that matter.

The rest is just details.

---

**December 26, 2023**

Thirty-two years ago, a nine-year-old dropped a Game Boy.

Today, somewhere, a geologist is plotting measurements on their smartphone. The data will upload to the cloud. Sync across devices. Export to their preferred software. All automatic. All modern. All sophisticated.

They probably don't know that twenty years ago, their professor plotted those same measurements on a Game Boy. That their textbook's stereonet figures came from Game Boy printouts. That someone's PhD thesis was saved by a video game cartridge.

They don't need to know.

The platform doesn't matter. The measurements matter. The understanding matters.

We built a bridge. Others walked across it. Some are still walking.

That's legacy enough.

---

Bob saves the post. Publishes it. Goes to make coffee (freshly roasted, single-origin Ethiopian).

While the coffee brews, he picks up the cracked Game Boy. Powers it on. The GeoCalc prototype boots. Some test data from 1992 still in memory.

He plots a point. Just one. Trend 045, Plunge 32.

The same measurement he used to demo for Maggie thirty-two years ago.

The point appears on the stereonet. Exactly where it should be.

Some things don't change. Some things work forever.

Bob smiles. Sets down the Game Boy. Goes to pour his coffee.

Outside, snow is falling on the Colorado foothills. Same mountains he's looked at for forty years. Same rocks. Same folds. Same faults.

Still there. Still measurable. Still mysterious.

And somewhere, someone is measuring them. Plotting them. Understanding them.

Maybe on a smartphone. Maybe on a tablet. Maybe on some device that hasn't been invented yet.

Doesn't matter.

The rocks remain. The measurements continue. The understanding grows.

That's what matters.

That's enough.
